### 1. Live AI overlay used in first patient pituitary-tumour surgery

**University / lab / researchers:** University College London and University College London Hospitals (National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery). Technical lead Sophia Bano (UCL robotics/AI); surgeons Hani Marcus and Danyal Khan. NIHR- and Google-funded.

During an endoscopic operation through the nose, a research AI system watched the live camera feed, tracked instruments, and colour-coded likely locations of hidden blood vessels and optic nerves around an 11 mm pituitary tumour. The patient, Rhys Hibbert, had been losing peripheral vision as the growth pressed on those nerves. Surgeons kept full control; the software acted as a second pair of eyes trained on hundreds of prior pituitary videos. After surgery he reported clearer sight and later returned to work. The team is moving from this first-in-human case toward a larger trial, and talks about a longer-term “surgical ChatGPT” that a surgeon could query or ignore.

**Capability:** Real-time video AI that flags critical brain anatomy so a surgeon can take more tumour out with less chance of hitting a vessel or nerve.

**Sources:**
- BBC News, Smitha Mundasad, 27 Aug 2026, https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjwg5n7y68xo
- The Guardian, 27 Aug 2026, https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/aug/27/london-neurosurgeons-ai-assisted-operation-brain-tumour

**Why independent:** BBC health reporter interviewed the patient at length and the operating surgeons; this is not a UCL newsroom rewrite. Guardian likewise reported the first-in-human use with patient and hospital quotes, not a press-release clone.

### 2. Engineered yeast turns PET bottles and crop waste into 3D-printed protein cookies

**University / lab / researchers:** Southern Illinois University Carbondale. Microbiologist Lahiru Jayakody; graduate student Sandhya Jayasekara; oxidative hydrothermal dissolution process from geology professor Ken Anderson. NASA Deep Space Food Challenge funding. Presented at ACS Fall 2026, Chicago.

The group breaks PET plastic and leftover corn stalks in hot, oxygenated water, then feeds the fragments to yeasts reprogrammed to treat that carbon as food. The yeasts make proteins, fats, acids, and flavour molecules. Mixed with starch, fibre, and sweetener, the paste is 3D-printed into cookies branded µBites. Production is about $60 per kilogram today. The cookies have scored well on aroma tests, but the team has not eaten them pending human-testing approval. Jayakody frames the work as a way to feed people in space, submarines, or disaster zones, and as one answer to plastic waste plus future food demand.

**Capability:** Converts common plastic and plant waste into printable edible protein, fat, and flavour ingredients using engineered yeast.

**Sources:**
- The Guardian, 24 Aug 2026, https://www.theguardian.com/food/2026/aug/24/cookies-plastic-astronauts-space-food
- Popular Science, Andrew Paul, 24 Aug 2026, https://www.popsci.com/science/3d-printed-plastic-cookies/

**Why independent:** ACS issued a 24 Aug presspac, which does not count. Guardian and Popular Science staff pieces add original interview material (cost, NASA three-year Mars framing, public-acceptance problem) rather than reprinting the presspac. PopSci is named specialist journalism with a staff writer.

### 3. Protein-interaction map of profound-autism genes for drug targeting

**University / lab / researchers:** UCSF Quantitative Biosciences Institute and Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences; Gladstone Institutes. Nevan Krogan, Matthew State, Belinda Wang, and colleagues. Science paper 27 Aug 2026. Rezo Therapeutics is a related spinout (mentioned in some coverage; this pack is coverage of the university map, not the company).

Hundreds of genes have been tied to profound autism, but that list has not yielded medicines. The UCSF team mapped how proteins from 100 high-confidence risk genes physically interact — more than 1,800 links, most of them new — and how patient mutations rewire those links. Different mutations often hit the same smaller set of processes (synapse building, which neurons form and when). CNN and NPR both interviewed the investigators and outside voices (Emory’s Fikri Birey; UCLA’s Daniel Geschwind; Autism Science Foundation’s Alison Singer; Autism Speaks’ Andy Shih). The pitch is that drugs usually target proteins, so a wiring diagram is more useful than a gene list. Three therapy programs are already being explored; everyone quoted stressed that human drugs are still a long way off.

**Capability:** A molecular wiring diagram of autism-linked proteins that points drug hunters at shared pathways instead of hundreds of separate genes.

**Sources:**
- CNN Health, Jacqueline Howard, 27 Aug 2026, https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/health/autism-treatment-new-map-study
- NPR All Things Considered, Pien Huang, 27 Aug 2026, https://www.npr.org/2026/08/27/nx-s1-5944507/scientists-map-the-interactions-of-key-proteins-linked-with-profound-autism

**Why independent:** National desks interviewed investigators *and* unaffiliated experts/advocates. This is not the UCSF School of Pharmacy newsroom post or a news-medical/EurekAlert clone.

### 4. Graphene “papier-mâché” coating for solvent-free battery electrodes

**University / lab / researchers:** Northwestern University. Chemical engineering professor Jeffrey Lopez. Presented Wednesday 26 Aug at ACS Fall 2026, Division of Energy and Fuels. ChemRxiv 2026, DOI 10.26434/chemrxiv-2026-lkcw.

Battery factories still paint electrode slurry on foil with a toxic solvent, then run huge ovens and solvent-recovery systems. Dry powder coating would cut energy, cost, and emissions, but it is hard to get an even sheet. Lopez’s group wraps metal-oxide cathode particles in a monolayer of graphene so they mix better with a tiny amount of PTFE binder — “papier-mâché-ing a balloon.” Lab electrodes reached about 10 mAh/cm², roughly three times typical commercial areal capacity, with about half the ion-flow resistance. Yonsei University’s Sang-Young Lee, not on the paper, told C&EN the low-friction graphene should also reduce equipment wear. Lopez said Tesla and others are already moving toward dry electrodes; this is a materials trick that could make that process work with less binder and, later, fluorine-free binders.

**Capability:** A graphene particle coating that lets lithium-ion cathodes be made as dry sheets, without solvent ovens, at higher energy density.

**Sources:**
- Chemical & Engineering News, Prachi Patel, 26 Aug 2026, https://cen.acs.org/energy/energy-storage-/battery-electrodes-graphene-coating/104/web/2026/08

**Why independent:** Named C&EN staff writer at the ACS meeting, with a same-day talk plus an outside Yonsei expert quote. Not a Northwestern newsroom reprint.

### 5. Small molecules that re-sensitize polymyxin-resistant bacteria

**University / lab / researchers:** University of Minnesota Twin Cities. Chemical microbiologist Erin Carlson. Presented Monday 24 Aug, ACS Fall 2026, Division of Biochemistry and Chemical Biology.

Polymyxins are last-resort antibiotics. Bacteria resist them by adding positive charges to their outer surface so the drug no longer sticks. Carlson’s lab screened more than 53,000 compounds for blockers of the two-component sensing systems that flip those defenses on, and found 2-aminobenzothiazoles that can restore polymyxin sensitivity 16- to 32-fold. The original idea was that they inhibit a kinase called PhoQ; in the test tube they barely do, so the real mechanism is still unknown. Wisconsin’s Helen Blackwell, not involved, told C&EN that hitting these non-essential sensing pathways is a hard but attractive way to slow resistance. The work is at the conference-data stage, not a drug.

**Capability:** Adjuvant molecules that make resistant bacteria vulnerable again to last-line polymyxin antibiotics.

**Sources:**
- Chemical & Engineering News, Max Barnhart, 28 Aug 2026, https://cen.acs.org/pharmaceuticals/antibiotics/antimicrobial-polymyxins-adjuvant/104/web/2026/08

**Why independent:** C&EN interviewed Carlson before her talk and independently interviewed Blackwell. Meeting news with named staff byline, not a UMN press release.

### 6. One-pot atom swap turns cheap isoxazoles into hard-to-make pyrroles

**University / lab / researchers:** University of Chicago, Mark Levin lab. Graduate students Abigail Bracken, Alexandra Lawrie, Isabella Romita. Talk and posters Tuesday 25 Aug at ACS Fall 2026; Nature paper the prior week (DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10933-6).

Pyrroles are common building blocks in medicines and materials, but many substitution patterns are expensive or unknown. Isoxazoles, which differ by one ring atom, are much easier to assemble. Levin’s group found a three-step, single-flask sequence that swaps oxygen for carbon: attach a three-carbon alkyne, open the ring with zinc, then close a pyrrole and spit out acetic acid. One example they highlighted drops a $2,300/g pyrrole to an $11/g starting material. KAIST’s Yoonsu Park and Manchester’s Michael Greaney, neither on the paper, praised the practicality and mechanistic depth. The team also built a model for when the ring substituents scramble, so other chemists can predict whether the reaction will stay clean.

**Capability:** A simple flask reaction that converts easy, cheap isoxazoles into valuable, hard-to-synthesize pyrrole drug/material building blocks.

**Sources:**
- Chemical & Engineering News, Brianna Barbu, 26 Aug 2026, https://cen.acs.org/synthesis/skeletal-editing-pyrole-atom-swap/104/web/2026/08

**Why independent:** C&EN staff interviewed the students and Levin at the meeting and quoted two unaffiliated skeletal-editing groups. Original trade reporting, not a UChicago newsroom clone.

### 7. 1970s fat-blocking compound makes mice lose weight without eating less

**University / lab / researchers:** University of California, Berkeley. Anders Näär, then-graduate student Justin Lee, Prabha Ibrahim. Science Advances paper (DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aed3119). Founders of ReRx Therapeutics (lead compound ReRx-001); still preclinical.

TOFA was invented in the 1970s to lower blood lipids. Berkeley researchers found that obese mice given TOFA lost fat without eating less, spent about 18% more energy in some tests, improved blood sugar and liver fat, and did not run a fever. The molecule both slows fat-making enzymes and partly turns on two fat-burning switches (PPARα and PPARδ). Combined with semaglutide or tirzepatide, mice lost more weight than on either drug alone. Mount Sinai hepatologist Scott Friedman, not on the study, called the dual action unusual and noted TOFA did not raise triglycerides — the problem that killed earlier drugs in this class. Näär warned people not to buy research-grade TOFA online; rat toxicity work is done, monkey studies are planned, human safety is unknown.

**Capability:** A small molecule that drives fat loss by raising energy use rather than by cutting appetite, and may stack with existing GLP-1 drugs.

**Sources:**
- Chemical & Engineering News, Ana Georgescu, 26 Aug 2026, https://cen.acs.org/biological-chemistry/biochemistry/TOFA-for-weight-loss/104/web/2026/08

**Why independent:** C&EN staff interviewed Näär and Lee and an outside Mount Sinai expert, and explicitly warned against DIY use. Not a Berkeley newsroom dump of the 21 Aug paper.

### 8. Lab-reared baby octopuses released to eat invasive Adriatic blue crabs

**University / lab / researchers:** University of Bologna aquaculture researchers Oliviero Mordenti and Antonio Casalini, with Emilia-Romagna regional government (Octo-Blu). Releases off Cesenatico and Riccione around 24 Aug 2026.

Invasive Atlantic blue crabs have wrecked Italian shellfish farms. Bologna scientists confirmed in tanks that native common octopuses will eat several crabs a day, then hatched larvae and released on the order of 130,000–150,000 millimetre-long paralarvae, plus artificial dens for shelter. They are boosting a native predator, not importing a new species. University of Palermo marine ecologist Gianluca Sarà, not on the project, told *Scientific American* the idea is powerful but impact may be “absolutely negligible” because ~99.9% of paralarvae usually die. New Scientist separately quoted Virginia Institute of Marine Science and University of Naples critics. Results will be judged with fishers later in the year; this is a field pilot, not a proven control method.

**Capability:** Hatch-and-release of a native octopus predator as a biological control for invasive blue crabs.

**Sources:**
- Scientific American, Gennaro Tomma (ed. Sarah Lewin Frasier), 25 Aug 2026, https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/scientists-just-unleashed-an-army-of-130-000-baby-octopuses-to-fight-off-a-blue-crab-invasion/
- New Scientist, https://www.newscientist.com/article/2586778-octopuses-released-into-adriatic-sea-to-tackle-invasive-crabs/ (opened; release dated 24 Aug in the piece; HTML did not expose a machine date)

**Why independent:** SciAm staff writer interviewed Casalini and an unaffiliated Palermo critic. New Scientist likewise quotes multiple outside marine biologists. Not a University of Bologna press reprint.

### 9. Personalized deep-brain mapping to treat chronic face pain

**University / lab / researchers:** Stanford University. Neurosurgeon Vivek Buch; Karl Deisseroth. Case series in *Brain Stimulation* (30 Jul 2026); *Science News* coverage 24 Aug. Outside comment from Stanford’s Michael Lim, not on the study.

Three people with severe chronic face pain had temporary electrodes placed through small holes in the skull. Over three days, surgeons tried different stimulation spots to map which patterns actually reduced that person’s pain. Two of three improved; one did not, which the team treats as useful (do not implant a permanent device). One woman in her 40s went on to four permanent leads and still reports relief at 6 and 12 months. The point is that chronic pain is not one circuit, so a short mapping admission can decide whether a high-stakes implant is worth doing.

**Capability:** A short, person-specific brain-stimulation map that predicts who will get lasting pain relief from a permanent implant.

**Sources:**
- Science News, Laura Sanders, 24 Aug 2026, https://www.sciencenews.org/article/brain-stimulation-chronic-pain

**Why independent:** Staff neuroscience writer at *Science News*, with an unaffiliated neurosurgeon comment and patient follow-up detail. Coverage date is in-window even though the journal paper is older.

### 10. Fruit-fly motor neurons help time the next muscle in a feeding sequence

**University / lab / researchers:** Peking University. Dong-Gen Luo; then-Ph.D. student Xiu-Wen Sui. Nature Neuroscience paper 24 Aug 2026. Outside comment from Maarten Zwart, University of St Andrews.

Motor neurons are usually described as wires that carry orders to muscles. In fly sucking/feeding, seven muscle pairs must fire in a tight sequence. Luo’s group recorded single motor neurons and found some of them also send a signal back that releases a “brake” on the next neuron in line — like a chain of dominoes. Luo suggested the same timing idea might help robots move more smoothly. Zwart, not on the paper, said the result ties together two previously separate lines of work and cautioned that flies are not a straight map to humans.

**Capability:** Evidence that motor neurons can sequence movement by telling the brain when to fire the next muscle — a possible control motif for robotics.

**Sources:**
- Science News, Yujia Huang, 24 Aug 2026, https://www.sciencenews.org/article/motor-neurons-muscles-motion

**Why independent:** Staff-written *Science News* piece with independent St Andrews comment, not a Peking University newsroom story.

### 11. 324-million-year-old Texas fossil shows early insects had 24 legs

**University / lab / researchers:** University of Cambridge paleoentomologist Erik Tihelka (lead); Dany Azar at Lebanese University / Nanjing Institute of Geology and Palaeontology. Nature paper 26 Aug 2026. Outside comment from retired Hampden-Sydney paleobiologist William Shear.

A West Texas shale fossil collected in 1985 sat in a San Diego museum drawer, misidentified as a crustacean. Tihelka found a drawing in a secondhand Russian textbook, travelled to see the “black smudge,” and under polarized light recognized insect anatomy — plus nine extra pairs of stubby abdominal legs with paddle tips. He named it *Chosha praecursor*. Related old fossils from Scotland and Illinois sit just outside living insects. The story is that insects did not jump straight to six terrestrial legs; they spent a long amphibious phase with extra swimming limbs, then lost them.

**Capability:** A redescribed fossil that fills a gap in how insects went from many-legged aquatic ancestors to six-legged land animals.

**Sources:**
- Science News, Elie Dolgin, 26 Aug 2026, https://www.sciencenews.org/article/insects-fossil-record-24-legs

**Why independent:** Staff feature built on Tihelka’s detective work and quotes two scientists not on the author list. EurekAlert/phys.org versions of the same paper are campus-style wires and were not used as sources.

### 12. Rubidium atoms in orbit fall at the same rate, to high precision

**University / lab / researchers:** Chinese Academy of Sciences Innovation Academy for Precision Measurement Science and Technology (Wuhan) and University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; Hefei National Laboratory; Technology and Engineering Center for Space Utilization, CAS. Ming Sheng Zhan, Jin Wang, Xi Chen and colleagues. Dual-species ⁸⁵Rb/⁸⁷Rb atom interferometer on China’s Tiangong / China Space Station. Science Advances report 28 Aug 2026.

Two clouds of cold rubidium atoms that differ by two neutrons were dropped in orbit for a quantum test of Einstein’s weak equivalence principle (different masses should fall the same way). Accelerations matched to about 0.05 thousandths of a percent. Space lets the free-fall last far longer than a tower on Earth, and atoms add a quantum check on tests previously done with metal cylinders. CAS authors describe a three-order-of-magnitude improvement over earlier microgravity atom tests and talk about future space-borne quantum inertial sensors.

**Capability:** A space-based atom interferometer that tests gravity’s universality and is a path toward quantum inertial sensors for navigation and fundamental physics.

**Sources:**
- Science News, Emily Conover, 28 Aug 2026, https://www.sciencenews.org/article/atoms-gravity-space-galileo-einstein

**Why independent:** Staff physics writer at *Science News* explaining a CAS/university space experiment for a general audience; not a CAS press-office reprint.
